Venturi Pressure & Velocity Analysis

Overview

I independently conducted a multi-stage airflow experiment using a converging-diverging venturi duct system to verify Bernoulli's principle — a foundational concept in aviation that explains how an aircraft wing generates lift by accelerating airflow over its curved upper surface, reducing pressure and creating an upward force.

Using water manometers and imperial unit conversions, I measured dynamic and static pressure at the inlet, throat, and outlet stages of the duct. Airspeeds at each point were then calculated using Bernoulli's equation and cross-validated with the continuity equation. Critical analysis was applied to explain any discrepancies between the two methods.

Methodology

The experiment was designed to isolate and measure the core variables of Bernoulli's equation within a controlled converging-diverging duct. Each stage was measured independently, with manometer readings taken at steady-state airflow. Readings were converted from raw water-column heights (in inches H₂O) to pressure values in lb/ft², then fed into Bernoulli's equation to solve for airspeed at each stage.

Results were then cross-validated using the continuity equation. Any discrepancies between the two methods were analyzed to account for real-world factors including duct wall friction, air density variation, and instrument sensitivity.

Bernoulli's Equation

P1 + ½ρV1² = P2 + ½ρV2²

Where P = static pressure (lb/ft²)  |  ρ = air density (slug/ft³)  |  V = airspeed (ft/s)

Continuity Equation (cross-validation)

A1V1 = A2V2

Where A = cross-sectional area (ft²)  |  V = airspeed (ft/s)

Pressure from Manometer Reading

ΔP = ρH₂O × g × h

Where h = manometer column height (in)  |  g = 32.174 ft/s²  |  converted to lb/ft² via unit factors

Set Up

The venturi duct was mounted horizontally with a fan driving steady airflow through the converging-diverging section. Water manometers were tapped at three measurement points — inlet, throat (narrowest cross-section), and outlet — to capture static pressure at each stage. All measurements were taken after airflow reached a steady state.

Results & Findings

Measurements confirmed the inverse relationship between static pressure and fluid velocity across all three stages. The throat — the narrowest point — recorded the highest velocity and the lowest static pressure, consistent with both Bernoulli's equation and the continuity equation.

Discrepancies between Bernoulli-derived velocities and continuity-derived velocities were small and attributable to duct wall friction losses and minor air density variation at ambient lab conditions. These deviations reinforced the practical limitations of idealized fluid dynamics when applied to real systems.

What I Learned

Within an enclosed, narrowing chamber, pressure and velocity are inversely proportional — a relationship that holds whether you're analyzing a venturi tube in a lab or the upper surface of a wing at cruise altitude. This experiment bridged the gap between textbook aerodynamics and what I experience firsthand as a pilot.

The pressure–velocity relationship demonstrated here sits at the core of fixed-wing flight. The lab results connected directly to concepts I apply in the cockpit:

  • Lift Generation: Aircraft wings (airfoils) are shaped so that air traveling over the curved upper surface moves faster than air beneath it, creating lower pressure on top and higher pressure below — the same principle observed at the venturi throat — producing an upward net force.
  • Angle of Attack: As a pilot raises the nose, the wing forces more air downward. This intensifies the pressure differential and generates additional lift — up to the critical angle where airflow separates from the upper surface and a stall occurs.
