Aerodynamics — Independent Experiment

Venturi Pressure & Velocity Analysis

Aerodynamics SJSU Bernoulli's Principle Fluid Dynamics Aviation Applications

Overview

I independently conducted a multi-stage airflow experiment using a converging-diverging venturi duct system to verify Bernoulli's principle — a foundational concept in aviation that explains how an aircraft wing generates lift by accelerating airflow over its curved upper surface, reducing pressure and creating an upward force.

Using water manometers and imperial unit conversions, I measured dynamic and static pressure at the inlet, throat, and outlet stages of the duct. Airspeeds at each point were then calculated using Bernoulli's equation and cross-validated with the continuity equation. Critical analysis was applied to explain any discrepancies between the two methods.

Key Findings

Results confirmed the inverse relationship between fluid velocity and static pressure across all three measurement stages — inlet, throat, and outlet. The throat, being the narrowest point, produced the highest velocity and the lowest static pressure, exactly as Bernoulli's equation predicts.

Aviation Applications

The pressure–velocity relationship demonstrated in this experiment sits at the core of fixed-wing flight. After reviewing results against Bernoulli's principle, I documented how the physics extend directly into pilot practice:

  • Lift Generation: Aircraft wings (airfoils) are shaped so that air traveling over the curved upper surface moves faster than air beneath it, creating lower pressure on top and higher pressure below — producing an upward net force.
  • Angle of Attack: As a pilot raises the nose, the wing forces more air downward. This intensifies the pressure differential and generates additional lift — up to the critical angle where the airflow separates and a stall occurs.

Methodology

The experiment was designed to isolate and measure the core variables of Bernoulli's equation within a controlled converging-diverging duct. Each stage was measured independently, with manometer readings taken at steady-state airflow. Results were then converted from raw column heights to velocity values using the full Bernoulli expression, then checked against the continuity equation (A₁V₁ = A₂V₂) as a cross-validation.

Discrepancies between the two calculation methods were analyzed to account for real-world factors including duct wall friction, air density variation, and measurement instrument sensitivity — reinforcing the practical limitations of idealized fluid dynamics models in applied settings.

What I Learned

Within an enclosed, narrowing chamber, pressure and velocity are inversely proportional — a relationship that holds whether you're looking at a venturi tube in a lab or the upper surface of a wing at 3,500 feet. This experiment bridged the gap between textbook aerodynamics and what I experience as a pilot: every time I adjust pitch, I'm directly manipulating the pressure differential that keeps the aircraft airborne.
